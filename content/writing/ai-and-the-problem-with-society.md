## OH NO! MY JOB!

Humanity fears what it does not understand. Now while that may sound ominous, in reality, it is a truth that has propagated centuries of technological growth.

Let’s hear it from none other than my boy Ari (Aristotle, the one from 350BC, yeah that one)

> “[If] the shuttle would weave and the plectrum touch the lyre without a hand to guide them, chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves.”  
- Aristotle on automation and technological advancement

Oh the humanity! So many jobs were lost because of these dastardly automations. Or, we could frame it another way. Someone just saved some people a whole lot of time.  
I am sure we are all familiar with the informal equation Money=Time. When we save others time, everyone gets a little richer.

For most of us, the kings of the past would wage war to live even 10% of the way that we do. Every restaurant, any piece of merchandise, entertainment, knowledge, conditioned air, running water, all at the flip of a switch.

So instead of running for the hills and screaming bloody murder about your job or chosen career path anytime some new technology comes out, just take a step back and look at the historical precedent. Telephone operators, Film developers, anyone who worked at BlockBuster (RIP), all of these are just a few example of modern dead jobs.

The core flaw of AI is that it will never, ever be conscious in the way that you and I are conscious. This is something that is beyond the natural sciences, and is even known in the scholarly communities as “the hard problem of consciousness”.   
As such, AI will always be playing catch up against us as we broaden our understanding of ourselves and surroundings and take on more complex problems.

You can point to a lot of things that can account for this cognitive decline, namely the pandemic, de-emphasis on critical thinking and problem solving, among others; but the newest rage has to be as we all know, ChatGPT.

Some like to draw the comparison between the effect of GPT on reading with the effect of calculators on Mathematics, and I think it is a poor comparison for one simple reason.

Creative writing is non-deterministic while math is deterministic.  
Given this discrepancy, it is easy to see where the comparison goes wrong.   
But there are in fact similarities in that just as teachers are outraged now, math teachers were outraged then.

So what happened? Where did all the outrage go? Well I will tell you. The math teachers adapted with the times.

When you have a technological advancement, your first reaction should not be “Welp this it, the technopocalypse is upon us everyone grab your pitchforks.” Rather, we should accept and welcome the tool as the new status quo, and as such **adapt our teaching style to match.**In the case of mathematics for example, as problem solving got easier, **questions got harder.**

### Solution

Now the issue that the teachers who deal with creative writing tasks have now is how do you increase the difficulty of a creative writing task when faced with an all encompassing non-deterministic text generator. Sounds a bit daunting when I put it that way, but I have a solution so hear me out.

GPT struggles with very very stringent numeric requirements.

For example, if you tell GPT to write a 376 word paper about Martin Luther King, where every other line starts with A or E, and ends with 3 sentences describing the paper, it will fail miserably.

The student would have to put in effort to A) fulfill the requirements, B) make it sound good, and C) make sure it makes sense.  
Now I will concede that the example above was a bit extreme, but you get the idea.

Another issue we must come to terms with is that in education, educators reward lower bound achievement and not so much upper bound.   
By that I mean students are rewarded for the bare minimum and rarely adequately rewarded for going above and beyond.

This grading style lends well to GPT, as rather than students putting in the bare minimum effort to fulfill the requirements and teachers attempting to detect AI (which in itself is quite difficult due to the non-deterministic nature of generative models) and it becomes this cat and mouse game, the student instead would have more incentive to go above and beyond in the utilization of his or her tools available at their disposal.

## Conclusion

The rise of AI technology signals a time for adaptation, not fear of a technopocalyptic scenario.

It presents opportunities for growth in both the workforce and education, encouraging creativity and higher-level thinking. Historical resistance to technological advancements, from Aristotle’s time to modern concerns about AI, highlights our capacity for adaptation (here we are now).

Educators can leverage AI in creative writing to foster student engagement and creativity, much like the adaptation to calculators enhanced problem-solving skills.

Embracing AI’s potential allows us to evolve and thrive amidst technological change, ensuring our success in an evolving world.
