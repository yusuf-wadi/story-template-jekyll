## **Practical Applications of BCI**

For a while I was obsessed with the idea of BCI everywhere in everything. I still am, but now the obsession is less naive and more refined. Let me explain.

I’d watch the Force in _Star Wars_ or the telekinesis in _Chronicle _(peak movie, do watch) and think, “Wouldn’t life be so much better if I could control everything directly with my mind?” That instinct still pulls at me, but building real BCI systems forces a different perspective. We already have high-bandwidth, low-noise ways of talking to our machines, so using brain signals for fine-grained control is like diving into the deep ocean to look for seashells that are going to wash up on shore anyway. Brain signals (especially with today’s noninvasive tech) are far too noisy and inefficient for something as simple as moving a joystick-controlled character. The interesting role of BCI isn’t as the main controller, but as the **substrate** that permeates and modulates all the higher-fidelity interfaces we already use. Instead of diving and getting lost, you watch the ocean from above, learning the currents so you can tell where most of the shells will end up.

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/0*SaKIRR8rEAarSel7.png)

So if brain signals aren’t for direct control, what are they for? I’ve been sketching out a theoretical interface in my head; something you could actually wear all day without looking like a cyborg. Picture this: normal-looking earbuds that sit in your ear canal, a slim wristband that could pass for a fitness tracker, and eye tracking built into a pair of lightweight glasses or even just your laptop camera. Each piece does what it’s actually good at, and the brain sensing acts as the maestro orchestrating in the background.

Imagine you’re working on a 3D modeling project. You look at a vertex (the eye tracking highlights it), you micro-tap your thumb to select (EMG confirms the click), but the brain band notices a P300 error potential; that little neural spike when you realize you selected the wrong vertex. The system auto-undoes the selection before you even consciously register the mistake. The brain data didn’t directly “control” the interface, it guided its trajectory, acting as a background error-correction layer.

The way I’ve come to see brain sensing is not as another control channel, but as a handshake between the harder signals. Eye tracking and EMG are loud and clear about _what_ you’re doing; the brain band quietly answers _whether you meant to do it_. A click doesn’t actually register unless your brain signs off on it. In practice, the interface would behave less like a trigger and more like a conversation. Your muscles and gaze suggest an action; your brain either nods or winces. The system listens for that nod before it treats anything as real input.

The obsession with “directly controlling everything with my mind” has ripened into something more refined: rather than replacing our interfaces, we give them a kind of peripheral nervous system. The brain band doesn’t take over but rather envelops the harder controls with currents of context. And as it turns out, the ocean is far more powerful than any single wave.
